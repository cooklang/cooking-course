# Module 2 · The oven

An oven cooks with hot dry air and a hot metal tray. It is slower than a pan and far more even, which makes it the right tool for things too big, too many or too delicate to stand over. The physics is the same as in a pan: food browns only where its surface has dried, and a crowded tray traps steam between the pieces. What the oven adds is time, and the question of when to stop.

That question runs through the module. A tray of vegetables is done when it looks done. A chicken, a piece of salmon and a tray dinner with meat on it are done at a temperature, and the lessons cook to a number on a thermometer, not to a time on a clock. Large things also keep cooking after they come out, so the roast chicken lesson includes the rest as part of the cooking.

Two lessons work on the meat before the oven is even on. The chicken is salted a day ahead, and the lesson explains what salt does to the structure of muscle. The salmon gets a fifteen-minute brine.

Oven temperatures are for a conventional oven, with the fan setting in brackets.

## The lessons

1. [Tray of roast vegetables](1%20Tray%20of%20roast%20vegetables.cook) is about dry heat and space, and about steaming dense vegetables under foil before they brown.
2. [Roast chicken](2%20Roast%20chicken.cook) salts the bird a day ahead, roasts it to 74 °C, and rests it.
3. [Slow-baked salmon](3%20Slow-baked%20salmon.cook) uses a low oven so the fish can be stopped at an exact temperature.
4. [Sheet-pan dinner](4%20Sheet-pan%20dinner.cook) puts the module together on one tray, with ingredients you choose, each added when its time comes.

## What you need

A large baking tray, a roasting tin with a rack, foil and a thermometer. The chicken lesson starts the day before.

## Safety

The salmon is cooked to 52 °C, which is a test kitchen's preference and below the USDA's 63 °C for fish. The lesson says so and says how to cook it to the official figure instead. Poultry is cooked to 74 °C throughout.
