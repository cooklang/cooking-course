# Module 4 · Eggs and emulsions

Two ideas share this module because one ingredient connects them. An egg sets: its proteins unfold and bond into a gel as it heats, and for egg white that happens between roughly 60 and 85 °C, far below the temperature of a pan. Cooking eggs well is mostly about stopping in time. An egg yolk also emulsifies: it contains lecithin, which lets oil and water stay mixed as tiny droplets when they would otherwise separate.

The first three lessons take those ideas one at a time. The omelette is setting egg gently and taking it off the heat before it has finished. The vinaigrette is the simplest emulsion, held together for an hour or so by mustard. The mayonnaise is a stable one, mostly oil, held by the yolk, and the lesson includes how to rescue it when it breaks. The carbonara needs both at once: egg that thickens a sauce without scrambling, and fat and water that stay together.

## The lessons

1. [French omelette](1%20French%20omelette.cook) sets egg in small soft curds and finishes it off the heat.
2. [Green salad with vinaigrette](2%20Green%20salad%20with%20vinaigrette.cook) makes oil and vinegar stay together, and treats three to one as a starting ratio, not a law.
3. [Mayonnaise](3%20Mayonnaise.cook) builds a stable emulsion drop by drop, and mends a broken one.
4. [Carbonara](4%20Carbonara.cook) puts the module together, off the heat, with pasta water.

## What you need

A small non-stick frying pan with a lid, a whisk, a few bowls, a large pot and a large frying pan.

## Safety

Three of these four dishes are, by design, below official advice on eggs, which is to cook them until yolk and white are firm and to use pasteurised eggs in dishes where they are not. The omelette is soft in the middle, the mayonnaise uses a raw yolk, and the carbonara's egg is thickened, not cooked firm. Each lesson says so plainly and says what to do if you want to follow the advice. It matters most for children, older adults, pregnant women and people with weakened immune systems.
