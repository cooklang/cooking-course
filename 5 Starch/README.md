# Module 5 · Starch

Rice, pasta and potatoes are all mostly starch, and they are all cooked by the same change. Raw starch sits in hard, dense granules. Heated in water above about 52 °C, the granules swell, lose their order and release a long-chain molecule called amylose, which thickens whatever liquid it meets. That is starch gelatinization. It is what turns a hard grain soft, and the same released starch is what makes rice sticky, pasta water useful and mashed potato gluey.

So the four lessons are four ways of managing loose starch. The pilaf washes it off and keeps the grains apart. The pasta lesson keeps it, in the cooking water, and uses it in the sauce. The mash avoids shedding it by cooking the potato evenly. The fried rice uses the reverse process: as cooked starch chills, its molecules recrystallise, which is called retrogradation, and that is why yesterday's rice fries as separate grains when fresh rice turns to mush.

One honest gap is stated in the pasta lesson. It is often said that starch makes oil and water emulsify. No source opened for this course shows that; the lesson claims only that starchy water is thicker.

## The lessons

1. [Rice pilaf](1%20Rice%20pilaf.cook) uses the absorption method, with the grains rinsed and coated in fat.
2. [Spaghetti aglio e olio](2%20Spaghetti%20aglio%20e%20olio.cook) finishes pasta in the pan with its own cooking water.
3. [Mashed potatoes](3%20Mashed%20potatoes.cook) slices the potatoes thin and puts them into boiling water, against the old rule.
4. [Fried rice](4%20Fried%20rice.cook) puts the module together with day-old rice and ingredients you choose.

## What you need

A saucepan with a tight lid, a sieve, a large pot, a large frying pan and, if you have one, a potato ricer. The fried rice needs rice cooked the day before.

## Safety

Cooked rice can carry bacterial spores that survive cooking. Chill it within an hour, keep it in the fridge, eat it within a day and reheat it once. The pilaf and the fried rice both give the rule.
